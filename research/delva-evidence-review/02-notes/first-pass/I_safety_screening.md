## Main evidence table (the 10 best items)

### Takeaway
Ten items carry most of the weight for Delva. They cover modern self-screening (ACSM 2015, PAR-Q+, CSEP GAQ), condition rules (Edwards 2023 on isometrics and blood pressure, the Canadian pregnancy guideline), pain monitoring (Silbernagel 2007), how safe exercise is (Niemeijer 2020, Keogh and Winwood 2017), the cost of having no supervisor (Lacroix 2017), and the regulators' wellness/device line (FDA 2026, MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1, MHRA). Two licence findings change the build plan. The 2023 PAR-Q+ is published under CC BY-NC-ND 4.0, and CSEP requires a licence and fees to put its questionnaires into software. So Delva cannot drop either questionnaire into a commercial app without permission.

### Inferences
- The two licence findings mean Delva needs one of: (a) a written licence from the PAR-Q+ Collaboration or CSEP, or (b) its own questions written from the open evidence (ACSM logic, guideline contraindication lists), probably checked by a clinician. Option (b) avoids fees and the "no changes" rule but loses the "validated tool" label. This is an inference from the licence terms, not legal advice.
- The evidence base is strongest for "exercise is safe enough that screening should be light" (Niemeijer, Riebe, Warburton). It is weakest for anything specific to unsupervised home bodyweight training: no per-hour injury rate exists for it.

## Screening flow recommendation (with evidence strength)

### Takeaway
Best current practice has moved from "screen everyone for risk factors and refer" to "ask about current activity, known disease and symptoms; send only the symptomatic, or people with known disease heading for vigorous work, to a professional". For Delva that means a short, mostly self-clearing onboarding screen, an intensity gate on interval work, special routing for pregnancy and recent surgery, and re-screening at each end-of-chapter check-in.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM 2015 dropped risk-factor profiling from screening because "CVD risk-factor profiling can lead to unnecessary referrals" and aimed to "reduce unnecessary barriers to adopting and maintaining regular exercise" — [Riebe et al. 2015 (URI record)](https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/kinesiology_facpubs/180); [ACSM blog](https://www.acsm.org/blog-detail/acsm-certified-blog/2018/02/01/exercise-preparticipation-screening-removing-barriers-initiating-exercise). **[Opinion/Industry: consensus]**
- The model uses three factors: current activity level; signs or symptoms and/or known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease; desired intensity. "Exerciser" = planned, structured moderate activity of at least 30 min on at least 3 days/week for at least the past 3 months — [Riebe et al. 2015](https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/kinesiology_facpubs/180); practitioner guide [Magal & Riebe 2016, ACSM's Health & Fitness Journal 20(3):22-27](https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/kinesiology_facpubs/179).
- Over-referral evidence: applying the 2015 ACSM algorithm to NHANES 2001-2004 adults aged 40+, 54.2% would be referred before beginning any exercise and 2.6% only before vigorous exercise. The algorithm referred fewer people than earlier questionnaires, and referral rose with age — [Whitfield, Riebe, Magal, Liguori 2017, MSSE 49(10):2056-2063 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28557860/). The authors say further validation is needed on whether it correctly identifies people at risk. **[Weak: cross-sectional modelling]**
- A UK university cohort (553 students) found the ACSM 10th-edition guidance cut medical referrals by about one-third — [OUCI record](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4MgaENP9). **[Weak]**
- PAR-Q+ developers report the new approach cuts physician referrals to about 1%, versus 15% with the original PAR-Q — HFJC abstract seen in search excerpts (exact article not confirmed; see [HFJC validation article](https://hfjc.library.ubc.ca/index.php/HFJC/article/download/151/110)). They argue "the health benefits of physical activity far outweigh the small transient risks". **[Weak: developer-reported]**
- PAR-Q+ structure: short form, then pages 2-3 with follow-up questions on specific chronic conditions that either clear the person or refer them to the online ePARmed-X+ — [Bredin et al. 2013, Can Fam Physician](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23486800/) ([PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3596208)).
- CSEP GAQ-P: "If you've answered YES to any question, you should consult with a healthcare professional before beginning or continuing to be physically active"; re-complete it if health changes during pregnancy — [CSEP GAQ-P UK version](https://csep.ca/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/CSEP-PATH_GAQ_P_Guidelines_UK_Version_V.2-1.pdf); [CSEP GAQ-P article](https://csep.ca/2021/04/18/the-get-active-questionnaire-for-pregnancy-breaking-down-barriers-to-prenatal-exercise/).
- Symptoms that should trigger stop-and-check under ACSM: chest, neck, jaw or arm pain or discomfort that may come from myocardial ischaemia; shortness of breath at rest or with mild exertion; dizziness or syncope; palpitations; unusual fatigue — [ACE form summarising ACSM screening](https://Www.Acefitness.Org/images/webcontent/assets/certification/ace-answers/forms/pt/21_Exercise_Preparticipation_Health-Screening_Questionnaire_For_Exercise_Pros.pdf); [ACSM consumer brochure](https://acsm.org/docs/default-source/files-for-resource-library/cpi-who-needs-a-stress-test-before-exercising-brochure.pdf). Primary ACSM Guidelines text not retrieved.

### Inferences (proposed flow; label = my recommendation built on the cited evidence)
1. **Age gate** 18-64 (Delva spec). Over-65s are out of scope, and referral rates rise with age (Whitfield 2017). **[Opinion, supported by weak evidence]**
2. **Symptom questions first.** Chest pain or pressure (at rest or on effort), fainting or blackouts, unusual breathlessness, palpitations, dizziness. Any YES: no workouts yet; ask the person to see a health professional, and let them self-clear after doing so (store the attestation). Mirrors ACSM "symptoms mean clearance". **[Opinion/Industry: ACSM consensus]**
3. **Known disease questions.** Heart, metabolic (diabetes) or kidney disease. A YES, with no symptoms, still allows First Steps / All Round / Move Freely at light-to-moderate intensity; vigorous intervals in Go Further (and advanced levels) are locked until the user confirms they have spoken to a professional. Under Riebe 2015 Figure 1 (as I understand it; full text not read this session), a non-exerciser with known disease is advised to get clearance before any program, and an exerciser only before vigorous work. **Decision for Delva:** follow ACSM strictly (gate everything for non-exercisers with known disease) or gate only vigorous work. The strict version raises the referral rate. **[Opinion/Industry]**
4. **Activity status question** (the ACSM "exerciser" definition) sets the starting level and how fast intensity rises. **[Opinion/Industry]**
5. **Routing questions (not ACSM-cardiac, but needed for an app):**
   - Pregnant now or gave birth in the last [N] months: route to a GAQ-P-style mini-screen (licence or own wording) and to non-supine, no-jump, no-breath-hold content. Delva has no pregnancy program, so the simplest safe option is to pause programs and offer Move Freely with a "check with your midwife or doctor" message. **[Strong guideline basis for the screening; routing is opinion]**
   - Surgery or a fracture in the last [N] months, or an injury currently under treatment: no program until cleared. **[Opinion/Industry: mirrors PAR-Q+ follow-up logic]**
   - High blood pressure, osteoporosis, back pain, knee pain: do not exclude; tag the profile for exercise modifications (next section). **[Strong to weak, by condition]**
6. **Re-screen** at every end-of-chapter check-in ("has anything changed: new diagnosis, pregnancy, surgery, new pain?"), copying the PAR-Q+/GAQ-P rule to re-complete when health changes. **[Opinion/Industry]**
7. **Messages:** say why the question is asked and that most people can start straight away. This keeps the screen from becoming a barrier, which was the explicit aim of both ACSM 2015 and PAR-Q+. **[Opinion]**

### Gaps
- No study found on the validity, completion rates or drop-off of *in-app self-completed* screening versus paper or professional administration.
- Riebe 2015 Figure 1 cell-by-cell wording was not verified this session; check it before implementing step 3.
- The PAR-Q+ blood-pressure question (I recall a resting BP threshold of 160/90 mmHg) was **not verified** this session.

## Contraindication/modification modelling

### Takeaway
For most common adult conditions (controlled hypertension, osteoporosis, low back pain, knee osteoarthritis) the guidelines say "exercise, with adjustments", not "exclude". Delva should model conditions as **tags that change exercise selection and cues** (exclude specific movements, substitute, cue, cap intensity). Whole-program exclusion should be reserved for symptoms, recent surgery and pregnancy complications. Each rule should carry an evidence label, because several popular rules (e.g., "no spinal flexion with osteoporosis", "never hold your breath with high BP") are label-practice more than trial evidence.

### Cited Findings
- **Hypertension and isometric holds.** Isometric training produced the largest resting BP reduction (-8.24/-4.00 mmHg) across 270 RCTs (n=15,827), ranking first for systolic BP (SUCRA 98.3%); isometric wall squats were the most effective sub-mode for systolic BP — [Edwards et al. 2023 BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/57/20/1317.full.pdf). **[Strong for BP benefit]**
- **Safety of isometrics in high BP:** 24 trials (n=1,143; mean age 56): adverse-event risk ratio 1.12 (95% CI 0.47-2.68, not significant), about 1 adverse event per 38,444 isometric bouts; events were mostly joint or muscle pain. Certainty very low to low; all trials at high risk of bias; most used handgrip, only 2 lower-limb trials — [Hansford et al. 2021, Hypertension Research](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41440-021-00720-3.pdf); [UNE record](https://rune.une.edu.au/web/handle/1959.11/31894). **[Weak for safety]**
- ACSM 2019 hypertension pronouncement: physical activity lowers BP in people with normal, elevated and high BP; FITT advice now includes aerobic, resistance or combined exercise on most, preferably all, days, totalling 90-150+ min/week — [Pescatello et al. 2019 MSSE (GWU record)](https://hsrc.himmelfarb.gwu.edu/sphhs_exer_facpubs/180); [ACSM page](https://acsm.org/?p=8812). **[Strong: umbrella review]**
- AHA 2023 update on resistance training (Paluch et al., Circulation 2024;149:e217-e231): resistance training described as at least as safe as aerobic exercise for people with heart disease and other conditions; most benefit at about 30-60 min/week — [ACC summary](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/ten-points-to-remember/2023/12/19/15/33/resistance-exercise-training). **[Opinion/Industry: AHA scientific statement]**
- **Breath-holding / Valsalva:** no primary guideline text on Valsalva was retrieved in this session (searches of the ACSM 2019 pronouncement and the AHA 2023 statement returned nothing on it). **Not verified.**
- **Pregnancy:** absolute contraindications in the 2019 Canadian guideline PDF (excerpt) include ruptured membranes, preterm labour, unexplained persistent vaginal bleeding, uncontrolled thyroid disease, and other serious cardiovascular, respiratory or systemic disorders; with these, women keep up daily activities but avoid exercise. Relative contraindications (e.g., recurrent pregnancy loss, gestational hypertension) call for a talk with the obstetric provider before moderate-to-vigorous activity — [Canadian guideline PDF](https://www.gov.nl.ca/tcar/files/Canadian-Guideline-for-Physical-Activity-Throughout-Pregnancy.pdf); [PubMed 30297272](https://www.pubmed.gov/30297272/). A secondary summary lists a different set (adds placenta praevia after 28 weeks and pre-eclampsia) — [Medical Dialogues](https://speciality.medicaldialogues.in/updated-guideline-for-physical-activity-throughout-pregnancy). **[Strong guideline; conflicting secondary reports, check the BJSM text]**
- WHO 2020 includes specific recommendations for pregnant and postpartum women and for people living with chronic conditions or disability; muscle-strengthening is recommended for all age groups — [Bull et al. 2020 BJSM (repository)](https://digibug.ugr.es/handle/10481/66573). **[Strong: WHO GRADE guideline]**
- **Osteoporosis (Too Fit To Fracture, Giangregorio et al. 2014, Osteoporos Int, doi 10.1007/s00198-014-2881-4):** GRADE + consensus. Without fracture: resistance training 2x/week (8-12 reps per set), daily balance training (15-20 min), spinal-extensor endurance work. After a vertebral fracture: moderate rather than vigorous aerobic activity and a physiotherapy consultation. The panel favours guidance on safe movement over blanket restrictions. All recommendations are **conditional** — [Osteoporosis Canada patient sheet](https://osteoporosis.ca/wp-content/uploads/2014-07-25-Are-you-too-fit-to-fracture-for-patients.pdf); [About Too Fit To Fracture](https://osteoporosis.ca/?p=3080). The specific spinal-flexion advice was **not retrieved**. **[Weak-to-moderate: conditional recommendations]**
- **Low back pain:** NICE NG59 recommends considering a group exercise programme (biomechanical, aerobic, mind-body or a combination) — [NICE NG59 recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng59/chapter/Recommendation). A vendor blog says NICE removed two psychological-therapy recommendations in a 29 July 2026 update — [Physitrack](https://www.physitrack.com/insights/nice-low-back-pain-guideline-update-2026) (**unverified secondary source; check nice.org.uk**). **[Strong guideline for exercise]**
- **Knee pain / osteoarthritis (NICE NG226):** not retrieved this session. **Not verified.**

### Inferences (suggested rule model; labels are mine)
Represent each condition as a record with: `screen_question`, `tier` (clear / modify / refer / pause), `exclude_tags`, `substitute_map`, `cue_ids`, `intensity_cap`, `recheck`, `evidence_label`, `source_url`. Exercises carry movement tags (e.g., `jump`, `supine_long`, `prone`, `loaded_spinal_flexion`, `rapid_twist`, `max_isometric`, `overhead`, `deep_knee_flexion`, `high_impact`).

| Condition tag | Tier | Rule (exclude / modify / warn) | Evidence label |
|---|---|---|---|
| Symptoms (chest pain, fainting, unusual breathlessness, palpitations) | Pause | No sessions until the user confirms professional advice | Opinion/Industry (ACSM consensus) |
| Known heart/metabolic/kidney disease, no symptoms | Modify | Cap at moderate; lock vigorous intervals until clearance confirmed | Opinion/Industry (ACSM consensus) |
| Controlled high BP | Modify | **Keep** holds (wall-sit, plank); audio cue "keep breathing, don't hold your breath" on every hold; avoid maximal-effort holds; no extra cap on hold length unless symptoms | BP benefit strong (Edwards 2023); safety weak (Hansford 2021); breathing cue = label practice, guideline text not verified |
| Very high or uncontrolled BP | Refer | Ask the user to get it checked first | Opinion/Industry (PAR-Q+ style; threshold not verified) |
| Pregnancy, no complications | Modify / route | No program by default; offer Move Freely or First Steps with substitutions (no jumping, no long supine work later in pregnancy, no breath-holding); prompt to check with a midwife/doctor | Strong guideline that activity is recommended; specific substitutions not verified this session |
| Pregnancy with any guideline contraindication | Pause | Daily activity only | Strong (Canadian guideline) |
| Postpartum | Route | Re-entry via First Steps; prompt about pelvic-floor and abdominal recovery | Opinion/Industry (Freeletics practice; CSEP postpartum GAQ exists, terms unverified) |
| Osteoporosis, no fracture | Modify | Keep strength and balance work; swap `loaded_spinal_flexion` and `rapid_twist` (sit-ups, crunches, twisting) for neutral-spine core work; add balance blocks | TFTF conditional (strength/balance); flexion swap = label practice, not verified this session |
| Vertebral fracture history | Refer + modify | Moderate intensity only; suggest physio consultation | TFTF conditional |
| Low back pain, no red flags | Modify | Keep exercising; pain-monitoring rule (next section); offer regressions | Strong (NICE NG59) for exercise; pain rule weak |
| Knee pain | Modify | Limit `deep_knee_flexion` and `high_impact` per pain rule; offer step-ups instead of jump squats, and similar swaps | Weak / not verified (NG226 not retrieved) |
| Recent surgery or fracture | Pause | Until cleared | Opinion/Industry |

- Show the evidence label in internal tooling. In the app, phrase rules as adjustments ("we've swapped jump squats for step-ups"), never as diagnoses. That keeps Delva within its "no rehab promise" and supports the wellness positioning (see the regulatory section).
- Delva's camera could help apply these rules (e.g., counting hold time, prompting breathing at a set interval). Claims about this must stay at "helps you follow the plan", not "keeps your blood pressure safe".

### Gaps
- No primary text on Valsalva/breath-holding guidance for hypertension was verified (ACSM Guidelines 11th ed., ESC 2020 sports cardiology are the likely sources).
- No direct evidence on BP responses to *bodyweight* planks or wall-sits in hypertensive people; Hansford 2021 is mostly handgrip.
- Too Fit To Fracture's exact flexion wording; Mottola 2019 warning signs and supine-position advice; NICE NG226 exercise advice: all unretrieved.

## Pain reports and stop rules

### Takeaway
Two kinds of rule are needed. (1) **Immediate stop rules** for cardiac-type symptoms, taken from ACSM and UK clinical sources: stop at once; seek urgent help if chest pain does not settle with rest, or if there is fainting or severe breathlessness. (2) **A pain-monitoring rule** for musculoskeletal pain, adapted from the Silbernagel 2007 model: tolerable pain up to about 5/10 that settles by next morning and is not rising week to week means continue or regress; anything else means skip that movement and suggest a professional. The muscle-soreness (DOMS) versus warning-sign distinction is widely used in practice, but I could not verify a primary source for its wording this session.

### Cited Findings
- **Pain-monitoring model (RCT):** patients could train at pain up to 5/10 provided it settled by the next morning; pain and morning stiffness were not allowed to increase from week to week. Continued tendon-loading activity under this model produced no negative effects versus rest, and both groups improved at 6 weeks, 3, 6 and 12 months (n=38) — [Silbernagel et al. 2007 AJSM (PEDro record)](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/18389); [MyOrthoEvidence summary](https://myorthoevidence.com/AceReport/Report/850). The paper says the model was originally described by Thomeé (**foundational**; the 1997 source was not retrieved this session). **[Weak-to-moderate: one small RCT, clinical population]**
- **ACSM warning signs:** pain or discomfort in the chest, neck, jaw, arms or other areas that may come from ischaemia; shortness of breath at rest or with mild exertion; dizziness or syncope; palpitations; unusual fatigue. Exercisers with such symptoms "should discontinue exercise and seek medical clearance" — [ACE screening form summarising ACSM](https://Www.Acefitness.Org/images/webcontent/assets/certification/ace-answers/forms/pt/21_Exercise_Preparticipation_Health-Screening_Questionnaire_For_Exercise_Pros.pdf); [ACSM brochure](https://acsm.org/docs/default-source/files-for-resource-library/cpi-who-needs-a-stress-test-before-exercising-brochure.pdf). ACSM consumer material also says to stop any exercise that causes pain and consult a health professional (same brochure). **[Opinion/Industry: professional-body guidance]**
- **UK (Moving Medicine, a UK clinical physical-activity resource):** "Stop immediately and seek urgent medical attention if you experience chest pain, blacking out or fainting, or severe or unusual shortness of breath" — [Moving Medicine: palpitations](https://everybody.movingmedicine.ac.uk/symptom/palpitations/). For dizziness: stop and rest, and wait until symptoms settle before doing more. For chest pain: call 999 if it becomes more frequent or severe, or does not go away within minutes of resting — [Moving Medicine: chest pain](https://everybody.movingmedicine.ac.uk/symptom/chest-pain/). (Exact wording comes from search excerpts.) **[Opinion/Industry: clinical patient guidance]**
- An NHS trust (Bradford Teaching Hospitals) tells patients to stop, sit down and not resume that session if dizzy or with chest pain or an irregular heartbeat, and that exercise can resume next day if the symptoms have gone — [Bradford Hospitals](https://bradfordhospitals.nhs.uk/?p=35704). Asthma + Lung UK lists chest pain or tightness, dizziness, feeling sick, clamminess or feeling very cold as reasons to stop and get advice — [Asthma + Lung UK](https://www.asthmaandlung.org.uk/living-with/keeping-active/keep-active-programme/exercising-safely). **[Opinion/Industry]**
- nhs.uk itself did not appear in the results; no NHS national page was verified.

### Inferences (proposed rules; my design built on the above)
- **In-session red-flag button ("I feel unwell"):** offers chest pain/pressure, faint/dizzy, can't catch breath, heart racing/irregular. Any choice ends the session (saved as partial activity, which fits Delva's "partial workouts are saved" rule, with no failure framing). Show "stop and rest; if chest pain doesn't ease with rest, or you faint or are severely breathless, call 999 / 112 / 911". Before the next session, ask whether they've had it checked. **[Opinion/Industry, built on ACSM + UK clinical guidance]**
- **Musculoskeletal pain report** (per movement, 0-10 plus "where"):
  - 0-2: continue.
  - 3-5 and settles by next morning: continue but auto-regress that movement next time; never add volume to "make up" (fits "missed workouts never become debt").
  - Above 5, sharp or joint pain, swelling, pain that doesn't settle by next morning, or rising week to week: skip movements with that tag for this chapter, offer swaps, and suggest seeing a physio/doctor if it persists.
  - **[Weak evidence: adapted from one RCT in tendinopathy; label as practice]**
- **Muscle soreness message:** reassure that general muscle soreness after new exercise is expected. Distinguish it from warning signs (sharp, joint-located, one-sided, swelling, night pain, numbness or tingling). **[Opinion: wording needs a verified source, see Gaps]**
- The camera and audio features should never "diagnose" pain (e.g., "your knee caving suggests a ligament problem"). Keep to "let's try an easier version" (see regulatory section).

### Gaps
- Thomeé 1997 original description not retrieved.
- No primary source verified this session for the DOMS time course (commonly given as peaking 24-72 h, e.g., Cheung et al. 2003 Sports Med; **not verified**) or for a validated DOMS-versus-injury triage wording.
- NHS back-pain red flags (e.g., cauda equina symptoms) not verified on nhs.uk.
- No evidence found on whether in-app pain check-ins change injury outcomes or adherence in consumer fitness apps.

## Injury risk evidence

### Takeaway
The data on serious harm are reassuring. Across hundreds of RCTs, exercise did not raise serious adverse events, but it did raise minor ones by about 19% (number needed to harm about 6). Weight-training sports run at roughly 2-4 injuries per 1000 h, and CrossFit/HIFT at about 3-4 per 1000 h, lower than common team sports. ED-surveillance data show calisthenics and HIIT injuries (knee and ankle sprains and strains) rose as these workouts became popular, but those data have no exposure denominator. **No study gives an injury rate per 1000 h for unsupervised home bodyweight training.** Supervision improves strength and balance results, but no study found compares injury rates between supervised and unsupervised training.

### Cited Findings
- **Adverse events in exercise RCTs:** serious RR 0.96 (95% CI 0.90-1.02); non-serious RR 1.19 (1.09-1.30); number needed to harm 6 (4-11); the authors call exercise therapy "a relatively safe intervention" — [Niemeijer et al. 2020 BJSM (NTNU)](https://ntnuopen.ntnu.no/ntnu-xmlui/handle/11250/2647230). **[Strong]**
- **Weight-training sports:** bodybuilding about 0.24-1 injuries/1000 h; strongman 4.5-6.1; Highland Games 7.5; rates low relative to common team sports; mostly retrospective (only 4 prospective studies) — [Keogh & Winwood 2017 Sports Med (Bond record)](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/the-epidemiology-of-injuries-across-the-weight-training-sports/); "approximately two to four injuries per 1,000 hours" for most weight-training sports is a secondary summary's description — [The Rheumatologist](https://the-rheumatologist.org/?p=14962). **[Weak]**
- **ED-treated weight-training injuries (US, 1990-2007):** about 970,801 weighted cases; mean age 27.6; 82.3% male; free weights in about 90%; weights dropping on the person about 65%; nearly 50% rise over the period, with the largest rise in incidence among people 45+ — [Kerr, Collins & Comstock 2010 AJSM (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20139328/); [Nationwide Children's release](https://www.nationwidechildrens.org/newsroom/news-releases/2010/03/new-national-study-examines-weight-training-related-injuries). **[Weak: surveillance, no exposure data; foundational]** Note for Delva: the main mechanism (dropped weights) does not apply to no-equipment training.
- **HIIT / calisthenics (NEISS 2007-2016):** 3,988,902 injuries from equipment (barbells, kettlebells, boxes) or calisthenics (burpees, push-ups, lunges); an average rise of 50,944/year that tracked rising interest in HIIT; knee and ankle sprains and strains most common; most injuries in men aged 20-39. The authors recommend preparticipation screening, neuromuscular training and pre-strengthening before HIIT — [Rynecki et al. 2019, J Sports Med Phys Fitness (Rutgers release)](https://old-news.rutgers.edu/node/137717); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2019/04/190409083239.htm). **[Weak: correlational, no denominator; full paper not read]**
- **CrossFit / HIFT:** pooled incidence 3.20/1000 h (95% CI 2.06-4.34; 14 studies) — [German J Sports Med 2021 meta-analysis](https://www.germanjournalsportsmedicine.com/archive/archive-2021/issue-7/musculoskeletal-injuries-in-crossfitr-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-injury-rates-and-locations/); range 0.2-18.9/1000 h, similar to weightlifting and powerlifting — [Rodríguez et al., Phys Sportsmed (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33322981/); HIFT overall 4.29/1000 h (3.35-5.23) — [JSOM 2022](https://www.jsomonline.com/Flipbook/Journals/2201-Spring/files/basic-html/page125.html). **[Weak: heterogeneous, mostly retrospective]**
- **Interval training and cardiac events (cardiac rehab, 4,846 CHD patients, 175,820 h):** 1 event per 129,456 h moderate-intensity versus 1 per 23,182 h high-intensity (3 events total; no myocardial infarctions) — [Rognmo et al. 2012 Circulation (Relias summary)](https://reliasmedia.com/articles/78350-safety-of-high-vs-moderate-intensity-exercise-for-cardiac-rehabilitation). **[Weak: very few events; supervised patients; foundational]** Relevant to gating Delva's Go Further intervals for people with known heart disease.
- **Supervised vs unsupervised:** supervised training improved balance and strength/power more than unsupervised in healthy older adults, with no difference in adherence; the advantage was clearest against fully unsupervised programs — [Lacroix et al. 2017 Sports Med (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28573401/); 2024 MA of 34 RCTs (n=2,830, 60+) agrees — [Sports Med 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-024-02024-1). **[Strong for effectiveness; no injury outcome]**
- **Home vs centre cardiac rehab (Cochrane CD007130, 2023 update, McDonagh et al.):** no evidence of a difference in total mortality up to 12 months (RR 1.19, 95% CI 0.65-2.16) or in most quality-of-life results; similar cost; "adverse events" were defined as withdrawal from the programme (figures not retrieved) — [Cochrane PDF](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD007130.pub5/media/CDSR/CD007130/CD007130.pdf); earlier version [Anderson et al. 2017](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/42517837/Anderson_et_al_Home_based_Versus_Cochrane_Database_2017.pdf). **[Strong for "home is not worse" in a clinical population]**
- **COVID-era home exercise:** the only figure found (at-home exercise ED injuries up "more than 48%" from 2019 to 2020; treadmills about a quarter) comes from a non-peer-reviewed insurer-affiliated report — [medicareadvantage.com](https://cdn.medicareadvantage.com/news/covid-at-home-exercise-injuries-report). **Excluded from the evidence base; no peer-reviewed study found.**

### Inferences
- The likely Delva injury pattern (from Rynecki 2019 and Keogh & Winwood 2017): knee and ankle sprains/strains from jumping and lunging, and shoulder and lower-back complaints from push-ups, planks and hinges. Camera cues and level progression should target these first. **[Weak evidence-based inference]**
- Delva can honestly say exercise has a low risk of serious harm. It should **not** claim its camera reduces injury: there is no evidence for that, and such a claim moves toward device territory.
- Lacroix 2017 suggests the real risk of unsupervised training is lower effectiveness, not more injury. Camera and audio feedback could be tested as a partial stand-in for supervision. **[Hypothesis]**

### Gaps
- No injury-per-1000 h figure for home bodyweight or app-guided training; no prospective cohort of fitness-app users found.
- Fisher et al. on supervision in resistance training not retrieved (search budget exhausted).
- No home-based exercise RCT adverse-event rates specific to unsupervised consumer programs; Niemeijer covers exercise therapy broadly.
- No peer-reviewed COVID-era home-exercise injury study verified.
- No verified per-1000 h figures for team sports to put alongside (Keogh & Winwood only state that weight-training rates are lower).

## Wellness vs medical device boundary (EU/UK/US)

### Takeaway
In all three markets, general fitness software is outside medical-device rules when its stated intended purpose is general wellness. Claims to diagnose, treat, mitigate or "restore function impaired by" a disease or condition pull software in. Delva's current spec (no weight-loss promise, no rehabilitation, no perfect-technique promise) sits on the wellness side. The features most likely to tip it over are: **pain triage that names a cause or condition**, **injury rehab or "return from injury" programs**, **condition-specific programs** (e.g., "for knee osteoarthritis", "for back pain", "for hypertension"), and **claims that camera form correction "prevents injury"** for a named condition.

### Cited Findings
- **US, FDA General Wellness guidance:** revised final guidance issued **6 January 2026**, replacing the 2019 version; keeps the two-part test (intended only for general wellness use, and low risk to users and others); excludes invasive products. Commentators say the revision broadens general wellness, including some non-invasive physiological sensors and BP-measuring products — [FDA guidance page](https://fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/general-wellness-policy-low-risk-devices); [FDA PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/90652/download) (could not be fetched; content confirmed through [Troutman](https://www.troutman.com/insights/fdas-2026-guidance-on-general-wellness-devices-policy-for-low-risk-devices/), [Ropes & Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/01/fda-adapts-with-the-times-on-digital-health-updated-guidances-on-general-wellness-products), [Faegre Drinker](https://faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/1/key-updates-in-fdas-2026-general-wellness-and-clinical-decision-support-software-guidance)). **[Opinion/Industry: non-binding regulator guidance]**
- FDA's acceptable wellness claim examples (earlier versions, via secondary summary): claims "to promote physical fitness, such as to help log, track, or trend exercise activity, measure aerobic fitness, improve physical fitness, develop or improve endurance, strength or coordination", and claims "to improve muscle size or body tone". Examples **not** general wellness include claims "to treat muscle atrophy" and to "restore structure or function impaired by disease". The second wellness category allows only "well understood" links between a healthy lifestyle and chronic disease, e.g., "promotes physical activity, which, as part of a healthy lifestyle, may help reduce the risk of high blood pressure" — [Cohen Healthcare Law summary of the FDA text](https://cohenhealthcarelaw.com/fda-publishes-draft-guidance-low-risk-general-wellness-products/). **Not verified against the 2026 text.**
- **US, FDA software policy:** "Policy for Device Software Functions and Mobile Medical Applications", dated 28 September 2022 (supersedes 2019). Software functions for individuals to "log, record, track, evaluate, or make decisions or behavioral suggestions related to general fitness, health or wellness" are listed as not devices — [FDA PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/80958/download); [FDA examples of software functions that are not devices](https://www.fda.gov/MedicalDevices/DigitalHealth/MobileMedicalApplications/ucm388746.htm); [FDA overview](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/digital-health/mobile-medical-applications). One secondary source says this policy is due for an update later in 2026 (unverified).
- **EU, MDR + MDCG 2019-11:** MDR Recital 19 says software for lifestyle and well-being purposes is not a medical device; apps that just monitor general fitness and wellbeing are not medical devices — [CMS law summary](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/digital-health-the-new-regulation-of-medical-software-and-apps). MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1 was published in **June 2025** (covers MDR and IVDR) — [European Commission update](https://health.ec.europa.eu/latest-updates/update-mdcg-2019-11-rev1-qualification-and-classification-software-regulation-eu-2017745-and-2025-06-17_en). Commentators disagree on how much Rev.1 changed: some say it tightened Rule 11 and modular-software guidance and added examples on non-medical software under Annex XVI; others say the qualification logic did not change — [Emergo by UL](https://www.emergobyul.com/news/european-revision-primary-software-guidance-mdcg-2019-11-revision-1-small-changes-meaningful); [OpenRegulatory](https://openregulatory.com/mdcg/mdcg-2019-11). **Primary Rev.1 PDF not read; conflicting secondary readings.**
- **UK, MHRA:** stand-alone software guidance (v1.10f; latest revision entry reported as July 2023) says monitoring general fitness, health or wellbeing is not usually a medical purpose, while software analysing health data can still qualify — [MHRA guidance PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/1105233/Medical_device_stand-alone_software_including_apps.pdf); [Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/health-data-analytics-software-might-fall-subject-to-medical-devices-regulation-says-uk-regulator). MHRA treats a clearly defined intended purpose as the foundation: "Failure to adequately define the intended purpose of SaMD will impair any subsequent efforts to comply" — [MHRA: Crafting an intended purpose](https://gov.uk/government/publications/crafting-an-intended-purpose-in-the-context-of-software-as-a-medical-device-samd). For AI scribes, MHRA's position is that using AI does not by itself make a product a device, but output with a medical purpose (e.g., a suggested diagnosis) does — [Brodies summary](https://brodies.com/insights/healthcare-and-life-sciences/mhra-clarifies-that-ai-scribes-are-not-automatically-medical-devices/). New UK SaMD/AIaMD rules are in progress — [MHRA change programme roadmap](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/software-and-ai-as-a-medical-device-change-programme/software-and-ai-as-a-medical-device-change-programme-roadmap).

### Inferences (feature risk map; my reading, not legal advice)
| Delva feature or claim | Likely position | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Rep counting, hold timing, form cues "to help you move with control" | Wellness | Fitness tracking and behaviour suggestions (FDA software policy; MDR Recital 19; MHRA) |
| Grouped programs by age/gender/level/goal | Wellness | No disease or condition input |
| Onboarding screen that routes users to "check with a professional" | Very likely wellness | Does not diagnose; only routes. Keep the outputs to clear / adjust / talk to a professional. |
| Condition tags that swap exercises (e.g., osteoporosis, BP) | Grey area; low risk if framed as preference/safety | Using health data to personalise could draw scrutiny (MHRA note on health-data analysis). Avoid "treats", "manages" or "for [condition]" claims. |
| Pain report that auto-regresses the plan | Wellness if it only adjusts training | Risky if it names a cause ("likely patellar tendinopathy") or tells the user how to treat an injury |
| "Rehab", "return from injury", "back-pain program", "knee OA program" | **Likely device** | Treatment or alleviation of a condition; Delva's "no rehab promise" rightly avoids this |
| Posture or form correction "to prevent injury" | Grey area leaning risky | General "move well" claims are fine. "Prevents [named] injury" or "corrects scoliosis/kyphosis" describes a medical purpose. EU MDR Art. 2(1) lists injury among medical purposes (diagnosis, monitoring, treatment, alleviation of, or compensation for, an injury); from my knowledge, the word "prevention" there is attached to *disease*, not injury. **Not verified this session; check [EUR-Lex 2017/745](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2017/745/oj).** |
| "Lowers blood pressure" (e.g., promoting wall-sits) | **Device risk** in the US unless framed under FDA's second wellness category ("as part of a healthy lifestyle, may help reduce the risk of…") | FDA example wording above |

- Delva's "does not promise weight loss, rehabilitation, perfect technique, or results by a deadline" rule fits all three regulators' intended-purpose logic. Keep one intended-purpose statement and apply it to the store listing, onboarding, push notifications and the audio coach scripts. MHRA stresses that the claims as a whole set the intended purpose.

### Gaps
- FDA 2026 general wellness text, MDCG 2019-11 Rev.1 examples, and the MHRA guidance's current version could not be read directly; check them before any claim review.
- EU AI Act intersection (pose-estimation AI in a non-medical app) not researched; out of scope here but worth a separate check.
- Consumer-protection rules on health claims (FTC in the US, CMA/ASA in the UK, the EU Unfair Commercial Practices Directive) were not covered.

## Gaps / open questions for Delva user research

### Takeaway
The biggest unknowns are behavioural (will users answer honestly, and does a screen put them off?) and product-specific (does camera feedback change injury or adherence?). The literature cannot answer them; Delva has to test them.

### Cited Findings
- Screening is meant to remove barriers, not add them — [Riebe et al. 2015](https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/kinesiology_facpubs/180); [ACSM blog](https://www.acsm.org/blog-detail/acsm-certified-blog/2018/02/01/exercise-preparticipation-screening-removing-barriers-initiating-exercise). Even the 2015 algorithm would refer 54.2% of US adults 40+ before any exercise — [Whitfield et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28557860/).

### Inferences (questions to test)
1. Onboarding drop-off: what share of users abandon at a 6-10 question health screen? What share answer "yes" to anything? Which wording lowers false "yes" answers (e.g., "diagnosed by a doctor" versus "do you have")?
2. Do users who get a "check with a professional" message actually do so, or just leave? Does a self-attestation ("I've checked") lead to more honest answers than a hard block?
3. Pregnancy: do pregnant or postpartum users expect a dedicated program? Is "pause and offer Move Freely" acceptable to them?
4. Pain reporting: will users rate pain 0-10 after a 15-20 min session, or only tap "this hurt"? Which prompt timing works best (end of session, next morning push, start of next session)?
5. Muscle soreness: do beginners mistake normal soreness for injury and quit? Test reassurance copy.
6. Camera and audio: do form cues change self-reported pain or minor-injury rates over a chapter (4 weeks)? This needs an internal study; no external evidence exists.
7. Re-screen at end-of-chapter check-in: completion and change-report rates.
8. Licensing: get answers from the PAR-Q+ Collaboration (eparmedx@gmail.com) and CSEP Permissions (info@csep.ca) on app licences and fees, then decide between a licensed tool and Delva's own wording.
9. Regulatory: have a regulatory professional review the intended-purpose statement, the pain-report feature and all condition-tag wording in the EU, UK and US before launch.

### Gaps
- No published study found on screening questionnaires inside consumer fitness apps (completion, honesty, effect on injuries).
