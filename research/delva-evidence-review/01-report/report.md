# Measure the user, not the demographic

Delva's strongest path is an on-device MediaPipe Pose Landmarker (BlazePose GHUM 3D, Apache-2.0 code and weights) feeding a small set of per-exercise, confidence-gated rules, validated on Delva's own consented recordings. That path is forced by two findings: every public dataset with form-error labels forbids or does not permit commercial use, and the only peer-reviewed test of phone-camera rep counting found an error of about one rep on five-rep sets, with results swinging on where the phone stood ([Oliosi et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12978916/)). The training evidence supports most of the 29 Oct 2026 spec: three to five short sessions sit above the two-days-a-week strength floor and inside the trial base of the new ACSM 2026 Position Stand ([Currier et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/)), the "sequence, not calendar" plan matches the best field experiments on flexible scheduling and on welcoming people back after a miss ([Milkman et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8822539/); [Beshears et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8734590/)), and the chapter check-in is a recognised behaviour-change technique. Two decisions are challenged: grouping users by age and gender has no support inside the 18-64 band and adds proxy-bias, inclusion and legal exposure, and 15-20 minute sessions cannot reach aerobic minute targets on their own. Textbook motor-learning rules that would ration feedback have shrunk to small or null effects after bias correction, so the real limit on audio cues is false alarms from an imperfect camera model. Privacy exposure comes less from the camera (if frames never leave the device and nobody is identified) than from check-ins, screening answers and the workout log, which EU case law treats broadly as health data. openGym is the most popular comparable open project, but it has no camera features, so it serves only as a yardstick for credibility and popularity.

**Scope, assumptions and method.** No clarifying questions were asked. Launch markets are assumed to be the EU/EEA, the UK and the US. Every star count, commit date, licence and citation count was checked on **2026-11-07**. Citation counts are Crossref cited-by counts (written **CR**) and Semantic Scholar counts (written **S2**), each labelled by source; Google Scholar was not reachable. "S2 not obtained" means the Semantic Scholar API rate-limited the request. GitHub contributor counts are the number of distinct commit-author emails in each repository's git history, an approximation (written "~N contributors"), because GitHub's contributor pages were not reachable. Several primary sources (some EU and US legal texts, the EDPB and ICO sites, some publisher pages) could not be reached; claims that depend on them are marked **unverified** rather than asserted. A first-pass research round was checked by a later verification round against primary sources; where they disagreed, the verified values are used here and claims that could not be found were dropped. openGym (AGPL-3.0, 20,026 stars at the user's check on 2026-11-07, last push 2026-11-06) is not re-analysed. Evidence labels used throughout: **strong evidence** (systematic review or meta-analysis of trials, large RCT or megastudy, official guideline, binding legal text, or official product documentation for a fact about that product), **weak evidence** (single or small study, preprint, observational or lab study far from Delva's setting, narrative review), and **opinion or industry practice** (consensus without systematic grading, store or vendor practice, commentary, or this report's own inference). This is research, not legal or medical advice.

**Evidence strength.** Strong evidence: the model cards and official documentation establish what each model outputs, where it is out of scope and how fast it ran on old devices. Strong evidence from peer-reviewed validation shows sagittal hip and knee angles in lab gait within roughly 2-8 degrees of motion capture (Stenum 4.0/5.6/7.4°; Washabaugh 3.7-5.1°), and a meta-analysis of markerless 3D gait capture rates hip and knee sagittal agreement moderate to excellent, ankle poor, and frontal and transverse planes under-studied ([Scataglini et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11175331/); CR 196, S2 183). The often-repeated claim that occluded-side errors are "roughly double" could not be found in that paper and is dropped. Weak evidence: MediaPipe's knee-valgus error of 18.83-19.68° in single-leg landings ([Heliyon 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11399566/); CR 12, S2 11), the confidence-gating study (n = 15), and an OpenPose camera-angle study that found significant biases between viewing angles and back views best ([Baldinger et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39943438/); CR 23, S2 21). Vendor fairness tables are a self-evaluation on in-house photos and conflict between Google's two cards; an independent Sony AI fairness paper could not be verified and is not used. Opinion: the camera framing proposals below.

**Key takeaways for Delva.** Use MediaPipe Pose Landmarker on both platforms so there is one skeleton and one rule set, with Apache-2.0 weights and heel points. Treat sagittal measures (depth, hip line, elbow angle, heel lift) as the cue-worthy set and treat frontal-plane valgus and lumbar rounding as coarse flags at most. Gate every cue on landmark confidence. Measure speed, heat and battery on Delva's own device matrix, because no benchmark exists for 2024-2026 mid-range phones or for 20-minute sessions; Qualcomm's AI Hub Models repo (1,257 stars, BSD-3-Clause; per-model licences not stated centrally) helps benchmark Snapdragon devices but is not a licence shortcut. Validate on the front camera, at home, on floor exercises and by gender, skin tone, age band and body size, because the model was trained on back-facing cameras and no study covers planks, bridges, dead bugs or bird dogs.

Use graded, specific, external wording and a short positive confirmation once a fix lands, without claiming learning gains. Keep audio latency for counts well under the ~180 ms average detection threshold, with pre-rendered clips. Put richer visual summaries between sets, where visual feedback's advantage can be used. Offer every cue through at least two channels (voice, watch haptics, large on-screen text), separate volume controls for coach voice, counts and music, and an easy mute, and measure mute and ignore rates as the field signal of cue fatigue.

**Evidence strength.** Strong evidence: relative-gain parity between sexes (three meta-analyses), trivial menstrual-phase effects, large individual response variability, small but real tailoring effects, and the proxy-bias mechanism. Strong evidence from the guideline itself: WHO 2020 gives one recommendation for "Adults (aged 18-64 years) including those with chronic conditions and those living with disability" ([Bull et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7719906/)), ACSM 2009 makes prescription "contingent upon an individual's target goals, physical capacity, and training status" ([ACSM 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)), and ACSM 2026 says untrained people benefit from various programs provided progression and adherence are core principles ([ACSM 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/)). Weak evidence: one non-randomised study found lean mass rose 5.24% in 18-28-year-olds vs 1.12% in 45-55-year-olds ([Phillips et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5621901/); CR 86), and men gained more VO2max for the same endurance dose in 8 small studies (+1.95 ml/kg/min) ([Diaz-Canestro and Montero 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31494865/); CR 69); both argue for honest expectation copy, not different programs. Self-selected intensity improves how exercise feels, with the gap depending on how hard the imposed session is (overall SMD -0.41; [Oliveira et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26300805/); [Ekkekakis 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19757863/)). Longer stated questionnaires lose more starters ([Galesic and Bosnjak 2009](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1093/poq/nfp031); CR 974); the oft-quoted 75% vs 65% figure is unverified. Legal points: GDPR Art. 22 (right not to be subject to solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects; Art. 22(4) bars such decisions on special-category data unless explicit consent or a legal basis plus safeguards) is verified against EUR-Lex ([GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679)); the AI Act's Recital 16 names sex and age among biometric categorisation categories, which makes inferring them from the camera to assign a program risky ([AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689)). The EU Gender Goods and Services Directive 2004/113/EC and the CJEU *Test-Achats* ruling (sex as an actuarial factor invalid from 21 December 2012) are **unverified** because EUR-Lex refused those pages; California's Unruh Civil Rights Act covers sex, gender identity and gender expression in business establishments per the state agency and statute pages read in the first pass ([CRD](https://calcivilrights.ca.gov/unruh/)), not re-verified; the UK Equality Act 2010 was not searched.

**Key takeaways for Delva.** The 29 Oct spec already defines content cells as program x level x days, so age and gender have no defined content effect; collecting them "for grouping" adds onboarding length, privacy surface and fairness risk with no benefit. Use age only as the 18-64 eligibility gate (and pregnancy, surgery and symptoms as safety filters, Area I). Make gender optional, inclusive and non-prescriptive. Assign by goal, level and days with a rule-based mapping, then let the first one or two sessions act as a camera-measured calibration that can suggest a level change the user accepts or declines. Explain each suggestion in one line tied to the user's own answers and allow switching any time, which also keeps decisions from being "solely automated". Audit adherence, completion and switch rates by gender, age band and body size, and watch whether women or 50-64-year-olds pick Build Strength less often under neutral copy and imagery.

ACSM 2009's "60-70% 1RM, 1-3 sets, 1-3 min rest" and Ebben 2011's push-up load percentages are unverified.

**Key takeaways for Delva.** Author programs in weekly sets per muscle (indirect work at half credit), progress by reps up to a ceiling of about 12 and then a harder variation, and cue effort "a couple of reps short of failure" rather than demanding failure. Use the first easier sets of the main movement as the warm-up and keep the cool-down short and honest (relaxation and range work, not "recovery"). For Go Further, prefer self-paced "hard but controlled" intervals with a steady alternative, and gate vigorous intervals per Area I. Frame the 8-week program as a first block with the next one ready, since gains fade when training stops. Be candid that bodyweight training lacks stand-alone trials; that is a reason to collect Delva's own outcome data, not to over-claim.

Weak evidence: Kaushal and Rhodes, Perri, fresh-start effects at temporal landmarks ([Dai 2014](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2014.1901); CR 299), habit of starting rather than of executing predicting frequency ([Phillips and Gardner 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26148187/); CR 119), short bouts giving no better 18-month participation than long bouts in women ([Jakicic 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10546695/); CR 402), beginners who chose the day's workout gaining more leg-press strength (+62 vs +16 kg, n = 16; [McNamara and Stearne 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20042923/)), and the HeartSteps micro-randomised trial, where suggestions raised steps by 14% (p = .06) with effects fading over weeks ([Klasnja 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30192907/); CR 240). The classic "about half of new exercisers drop out within 6 months" traces to early 1980s studies as reviewed by [Rhodes and Sui 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8450373/), not to a verified primary figure. Papers on streak dependency, the "hidden cost" of quantification, mid-goal dips and post-milestone let-down (Stawarz 2015, Renfree 2016, Etkin 2016, Bonezzi 2011, Amir and Ariely 2008) were confirmed only bibliographically; their findings were not re-checked and are treated as hypotheses. The "what-the-hell" effect source could not be found.

**Key takeaways for Delva.** Put micro-milestones inside chapter 1 (first workout, first week) and an early light check-in after three workouts or seven days. Pair the calendar-free sequence with an optional user-chosen "when and where" plan and a friendly return message after a miss. If streaks are shown at all, make them weekly, count approved shorter versions, and offer repair. Give the shorter-version "reserve" a small visible limit. Instrument attrition from day 0 (camera setup, permissions, first and second workout, chapter boundaries) and plot curves, because none of this evidence comes from vision-based home fitness apps.

**Evidence strength.** Strong evidence: harm rates (Niemeijer), blood-pressure benefits of isometrics (Edwards), supervised-vs-unsupervised effectiveness, the pregnancy guideline's overall conclusion, and the FDA texts. Weak evidence: injury epidemiology, which mostly lacks exposure data: weight-training sports range from 0.24-1 injuries per 1,000 h (bodybuilding) to 7.5 (Highland Games) ([Keogh and Winwood 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27328853/); CR 165), CrossFit 0.2-18.9 per 1,000 h ([Rodríguez 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33322981/)), and an estimated 3,988,903 US emergency visits from HIIT-type equipment and calisthenics in 2007-2016, most often in men (58%) and people aged 20-39 (39%) ([Rynecki 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30758171/)). In cardiac rehab, events ran at 1 per 129,456 h of moderate vs 1 per 23,182 h of high-intensity exercise, with only 3 events and no heart attacks ([Rognmo 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22879367/); CR 407), which supports gating vigorous intervals for known heart disease. Opinion or consensus: ACSM screening itself, Too Fit To Fracture's conditional osteoporosis advice ([Giangregorio 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00198-014-2881-4)), and UK clinical stop-rule wording. No study gives an injury rate per 1,000 hours for unsupervised home bodyweight training, and no study tests in-app screening.

**Key takeaways for Delva.** Screen in a few questions: symptoms first (any yes pauses training until the user confirms professional advice, stored as a self-attestation), known heart, metabolic or kidney disease (allow light-to-moderate programs, lock vigorous intervals until clearance is confirmed; Delva must decide whether to follow ACSM strictly for non-exercisers), activity status, and routing for pregnancy, recent surgery or fracture. Re-screen at every chapter check-in. Model conditions as tags that swap or cap exercises rather than exclude whole programs. Add an "I feel unwell" button that ends the session (saved as partial activity) with plain stop-and-seek-help advice. Keep every claim at "helps you follow the plan": no rehab, no condition-specific programs, no "prevents injury" or "lowers blood pressure" claims, which would move Delva toward device rules.

**Key takeaways for Delva.** Keep frames in memory on the device and never write, log or upload them; keep keypoints in a short rolling window and drop face and hand landmarks inside the inference wrapper; store only derived metrics by default. Never identify users by face or movement (use explicit profile selection). Ask age and gender by form, never infer them or emotions from the camera. Make recording, upload and model-training use separate opt-ins with a visible indicator and short retention. Collect health data (check-ins, screening) only after a dedicated, granular, withdrawable explicit consent, evaluate screening on device where possible, and never hard-block automatically. Keep health data out of iCloud and away from ad or analytics SDKs, disclose any third-party AI use, run a DPIA before launch, offer a camera-free mode, and add network tests in CI that prove camera sessions send no image or keypoint traffic.

## 4. Evidence that challenges or supports Delva's current decisions

None of the spec's specific numbers (8 weeks, about two weeks per chapter, 14 active days, 3/4/5 days, 15-20 minutes) has been tested head-to-head in a fitness app. The verdicts below say whether each choice sits inside or against the evidence.

| Decision (spec of 29 Oct 2026) | Verdict | Evidence strength | What supports it | What challenges it | Suggested adjustment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **8-week minimum in 4 chapters** | **Supported** as a reasonable first block | Weak to moderate (indirect) | ACSM 2026 included only interventions ≥6 weeks with ≥12 sessions; 8 weeks at ≥3 days gives ≥24 sessions, inside that base ([ACSM 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/)). Strength gains within 4 weeks of a push-up ladder ([Kotarsky](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29466268/)); range-of-motion gains from ≥2 weeks of stretching ([Konrad](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37301370/)). Periodisation does not consistently matter, so chapters can be motivational units. Median habit formation 59-66 days ([Singh 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39685110/)). Self-monitoring combined with goal review is an effective technique ([Michie 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19916637/)). | Median retention in app studies is 5.5 days, far shorter than chapter 1 ([Pratap](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32128451/)); habit ranges 4-335 days; gains fade when training stops ([Bosquet](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347054/)). Post-milestone and mid-goal dips are plausible but their sources were not re-checked. | Add day-scale milestones inside chapter 1; have the next chapter and next program ready at each boundary; never claim "habit in 8 weeks". |
| **Chapter ends only when work is done AND ≥14 active days have passed** | **Untested; neutral** (a pacing rule, not a physiological need) | Weak / opinion | Paused days not counting avoids self-blame framing that worsens broken-streak effects ([Silverman and Barasch](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1093/jcr/ucac029)); graded tasks are linked with maintained change ([Howlett](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29506209/)). | No study supports a 14-day floor; volume-equated 6 vs 3 days a week did not harm gains in trained men ([Šarić](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30363041/)); a fast finisher may feel held back (no evidence either way). | Show both counters ("work 9 of 12, days 10 of 14"); give early finishers optional extra or shorter sessions that count as activity; test how users understand "active day". |
| **3, 4 or 5 starting days a week** | **Supported** | Strong for "≥2 days and volume matter"; weak for this exact menu | Guideline floor is ≥2 strength days ([WHO 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7719906/); ACSM 2026). Frequency does not change hypertrophy when volume is equal, so people can choose by preference ([Schoenfeld 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30558493/)); frequency helps strength with diminishing returns ([Pelland](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/)). Prescribing 5-7 vs 3-4 days raised total exercise without lowering adherence ([Perri 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12211512/)). At least 4 sessions a week for 6 weeks built gym habits ([Kaushal and Rhodes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25851609/)). | WHO found no dose-response for health from more strength volume, so do not promise more from 5 than 3 days. ACSM 2009 suggested 2-3 days for novices, though ACSM 2026 notes that advice rested on 3 studies (n = 59). Higher prescribed intensity lowered adherence (Perri). Stretching frequency is contested ([Konrad](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37301370/) vs [Thomas](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29506306/)). | Keep 3/4/5; consider an "N + reserve" framing for 4-5 days; keep beginner 5-day cells lighter per session; let check-ins treat frequency as a volume decision. |
| **15-20 minute sessions with warm-up, main work and cool-down** | **Supported for strength and health; challenged as a complete aerobic dose** | Strong (both directions); weak for an adherence advantage | 3-4 sessions with 10-14 minutes of main work give ~30-55 strength minutes a week, inside the 30-60 min/week band of lowest risk ([Momma 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35228201/)); bouts of any length count and the least active benefit most ([PAG 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30418471/)); warm-ups help performance ([Fradkin](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19996770/)). | 5 x 20 minutes is 100 minutes of total session time, below 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity (arithmetic against PAG/WHO). Short bouts did not improve 18-month adherence vs long bouts ([Jakicic 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10546695/)). ACSM 2026 links hypertrophy to ≥10 weekly sets per muscle, which is hard to reach for every major muscle in short sessions ([ACSM 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/)). | Keep 15-20 minutes; prioritise multi-joint moves; say sessions "count toward" weekly activity; consider letting users log walks; measure completion by length. |
| **Grouping users by age and gender** | **Challenged** | Strong | None for prescription within 18-64. | Similar relative gains between sexes ([Roberts](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32218059/); [Refalo](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40028215/); [Jones](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33332016/)); one WHO recommendation for all adults 18-64; ACSM prescribes by status, goal and capacity; individual variability exceeds group averages ([Ross 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30862704/)); demographic proxies cause bias ([Obermeyer](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31649194/)); binary content-changing gender fields harm trans and non-binary users; GDPR Art. 22 and the AI Act's categorisation rules add risk; the spec's content cells do not use age or gender. | Use age only as the eligibility gate; make gender optional and non-prescriptive; personalise on goal, measured level, days, preferences and observed response; audit outcomes by subgroup. |
| **Sequence-based plan: missed workouts never become debt** | **Supported** | Moderate to strong for the principle; weak for the exact mechanism | Flexible incentives beat fixed time slots during and after ([Beshears](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8734590/)); rewarding a return after a miss was the best of 54 interventions ([Milkman](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8822539/)); "missing one opportunity" did not materially affect habit formation ([Lally](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1002/ejsp.674)); 3-week breaks left final gains unchanged in young men ([Ogasawara](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23053130/)); choosing the day's workout did not hurt beginners ([McNamara and Stearne](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20042923/)). openGym also keeps the next workout next (comparison only). | No calendar means no built-in time cue, and consistency predicts habit ([Kaushal and Rhodes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25851609/)); losses grow with break length and are larger over 65 and in the inactive ([Bosquet](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347054/)). No trial compares sequence and calendar plans. | Add an optional "when and where" plan and a warm return message; after long pauses (a design threshold of roughly 2-3 weeks, untested) offer a one-step-easier welcome-back session. |
| **Credit for approved shorter versions; none for unapproved removal; partial workouts saved as activity** | **Supported** in principle | Moderate for the principle; weak for the rules | Emergency reserves with a cost raise preference and persistence ([Sharif and Shu 2017](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1509/jmr.15.0231)); repairable streaks soften lapses; "some is better than none" (WHO, PAG); one hard set per exercise 1-3 times a week raised strength in trained men ([Androulakis-Korakakis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-019-01236-0)). | Unlimited free shorter versions may become the norm (inference from Sharif and Shu); camera counts err by about a rep on short sets ([Oliosi](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12978916/)), so a detector must never be the sole judge of "done". | Give a small visible reserve per chapter; keep at least one hard set per main movement in short versions; allow one-tap count correction; never let form cues affect credit. |
| **End-of-chapter check-in suggesting frequency changes (user accepts or declines)** | **Supported** | Moderate for the technique; weak for cadence and wording | Self-monitoring plus goal review: 0.42 vs 0.26 ([Michie 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19916637/)); action planning and graded tasks linked with maintenance ([Howlett](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29506209/)); user choice keeps the suggestion from being "solely automated" (GDPR Art. 22). | A two-week cadence misses the first-week dropout window ([Pratap](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32128451/)); prompt effects fade over weeks ([Klasnja](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30192907/)). | Add a light first check-in after 3 workouts or 7 days; frame "fewer days" as fitting the plan to life, not failure; re-screen health at every check-in (Area I). |
